Your Watch Box Is Not a Savings Account, and Mine Wasn't Either

General finance tips from a collector who learned the hard way: build cash, kill debt, buy index funds, and stop treating watches as savings.

The general finance tips that work are boring. Keep real cash for emergencies, pay off high-interest debt, automate cheap index investing, and buy watches with whatever's left. I learned that the stupid way. In April 2022 I sat in a dealer parking lot in Chicago around 9 p.m., refreshing Chrono24 on my phone. I was sure my Datejust 41 was worth $14,200, and in my head I'd already spent the profit on a second piece. Six months later comparable listings sat closer to $10,500, and the 'profit' I'd already put on a credit card was gone. So here's my position, and I'll defend it below: your watch box is a hobby, not a balance sheet, and treating it like one is how collectors get hurt.

I've spent about fifteen years buying, selling and occasionally regretting watches. In that time I've watched plenty of people in collector forums and at local get-togethers make the same mistake. They know lug-to-lug measurements and service intervals cold but have only a vague idea of their own emergency fund. The fixes aren't complicated. They're just unglamorous, which is why people skip them.

Build the Cash Buffer Before the Next Grail

Start with the money nobody gets excited about. The Federal Reserve's Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2024 report found that 63% of adults said they'd cover a hypothetical $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. Flip that around and more than a third of people would borrow, sell something, or not manage at all. For collectors, 'sell something' usually means dumping a watch at a bad price in a bad week.

Aim for three to six months of essential expenses in a separate high-yield savings account. Keep it out of your checking account, where it quietly turns into lunch. Many online banks paid around 4% APY through much of 2025. Even if rates drift lower, that beats the 0.01% a big-bank savings account pays.

But here's what most advice skips... the buffer protects your collection too. In 2019 I had to replace a transmission for $3,800, in the same month my Speedmaster went in for service. I had the cash, so nothing got sold. That's what the buffer is for.

Kill Expensive Debt, Including the Friendly-Looking Kind

Credit card debt is the fastest way to make a watch cost twice its sticker price. The Federal Reserve's G.19 consumer credit release shows the average rate on card accounts actually charged interest ran north of 21% through 2025. No investment you'll find reliably beats that. Paying the card down is a guaranteed return at that rate.

The 0% financing problem

Plenty of authorized dealers and grey sellers now offer 12 or 24 month promotional financing through third-party lenders. That's fine if you'd have paid cash anyway and you clear the balance on time. In my experience, though, it mostly lets people buy a watch they couldn't afford. Check the terms for deferred interest. With deferred interest, missing the payoff date by one day can charge you all the interest back to the first month. For more on this, see The $400 Question Most Watch Collectors Can't Answer.

My rule is plain. If I can't write the check today, I can't buy the watch today. I've broken that rule once and paid around $640 in interest on a Grand Seiko I sold within a year anyway.

Index Funds Will Beat Your Stock Picks, and Mine

Once the cash is set aside and the cards are clean, automate investing into low-cost, broad index funds. I resisted this for years because picking stocks felt like picking watches: hours of research plus a bit of swagger. The data doesn't care about swagger. S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA U.S. Scorecard has shown year after year that roughly nine in ten actively managed U.S. large-cap funds trail the S&P 500 over 15-year periods. Professionals with research teams lose to a cheap index most of the time. You and I won't do better on our lunch breaks.

What automation looks like in practice

  • Contribute enough to get any employer 401(k) match first, since the match is an instant return.
  • Set a fixed transfer into an IRA or brokerage account for the day after payday.
  • Pick a total market or S&P 500 fund with an expense ratio under 0.10%.
  • Raise your contribution by 1% every time you get a raise.

I wrote more about the habits that compound in finance tips that build real wealth instead of the illusion of it. For the short list of moves that make a real difference, see finance tips that actually move the needle.

Where Watches Actually Fit in Your Money

This is where collectors genuinely disagree. Some swear certain references are investments, and a few have been. Most aren't. Secondary prices tracked by WatchCharts fell hard after the spring 2022 peak, and plenty of steel sports models still sit well below those highs. When you sell, dealer spreads take 15% to 25%. Every five to ten years you also pay $700 to $1,200 for a service. After both, the 'appreciation' often disappears. Buy watches you'd happily wear if they were worth zero.

Okay, confession. For about two years I kept a Google Sheet called 'Collection NAV' with roughly live prices for every watch I owned. I checked it more often than my actual retirement account. My friend Dev did the same thing and took it further. He'd text me screenshots when his Explorer went up $200, like he'd closed a deal. Neither of us ever sold at the top. I'm not totally sure the spreadsheet hurt us. Maybe it kept us disciplined? Probably not. Mostly it was a slow way to feel rich.

So budget for the hobby the way you'd budget for travel. Put a fixed share of take-home pay, maybe 5%, into a dedicated account, and buy watches only from that account. I broke down the collector-specific version in finance tips that work for watch collectors and everyone else. And it's fine to buy nothing for a year. The watches will still be there... usually cheaper. For more on this, see Rolex Bought Bucherer and the Watch World Still Hasn't Processed It.

Before you close this tab, open your bank app and set up one automatic transfer. Pick a fixed amount, even $150, and send it to a separate high-yield savings account the day after every paycheck. Name the account something blunt, like 'Not Watches.' Leave it alone until it holds three months of expenses. Only then start funding the hobby account.

Frequently asked questions

Are luxury watches a good investment?

Usually not. Dealer spreads of 15% to 25% and service costs of $700 or more eat most gains, and secondary prices fell sharply after 2022. Buy watches to wear and invest through low-cost index funds.

How much emergency savings should I have before buying a watch?

Aim for three to six months of essential expenses in a separate high-yield savings account. Once that's funded and you have no credit card debt, put a fixed share of your pay toward the hobby.

Is 0% financing on a watch a bad idea?

It's only safe if you'd have paid cash anyway and you clear the balance before the promo ends. Many plans use deferred interest, which charges all the interest back to the first month if you miss the payoff date.
