Pass the $400 Test Before You Buy Another Watch

General finance tips from a watch collector who learned the hard way: build a cash buffer, clear card debt, automate investing, then buy the watch.

The finance tips that matter most are boring, and they only work in order: cash buffer first, high-interest debt second, automatic investing third, fun money last. If you skip the order, the fun money ends up costing you double. In March 2019 I put a Black Bay 58 on a credit card at 23.99% APR and told myself I'd clear it in three months. It took eleven. By the end I'd paid the bank roughly $410 in interest, which is about the price of a good strap and a service deposit, and I still wince when I look at that watch. Order beats intensity. You don't need a stricter budget. You need to do things in the right sequence.

The bar is lower than most people think, and a lot of people still miss it. The Federal Reserve's Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households covering 2024 found that only 63% of adults would pay a surprise $400 expense with cash or its equivalent. So more than a third of people would need a card, a loan or a favor to cover a dead car battery and a tow. If that's you, the next watch can wait. Everything below is about getting you past that line and then well beyond it.

Build the Boring Buffer Before Anything With a Rotor

Before you think about index funds or the panda dial you keep refreshing on Chrono24, you need cash that sits there and does nothing exciting. Three months of essential expenses in a high-yield savings account is the floor. That means essentials, not your full lifestyle: rent, groceries, insurance and minimum payments, the bills that sink you if your income stops.

When I finally built mine in 2020, the number came to $9,600. Leaving that much idle felt ridiculous. Then my car's transmission failed that October and cost $3,850. I paid it from the buffer, refilled it over five months and never touched a card. That's the point of the buffer. It turns an emergency into an annoyance.

Where to park it

Don't leave it at a big branch bank paying almost nothing. The FDIC's national average savings rate has stayed well under 1%, while online banks have paid several times that. On a $10,000 buffer, the difference is a few hundred dollars a year for about ten minutes of paperwork. For more on this, see The $400 Test Most Watch Buyers Fail Before Their First Grail.

Treat Card Debt Like a Fake in Your Collection

Here's my position, and some planners will push back on it: any card balance above 15% APR is an emergency, and it comes before investing. No index fund reliably returns 22% a year. Paying off a card charging 22% gets you that return with certainty, and it's tax-free.

The real argument is about method. Two approaches dominate:

  • Avalanche: you pay the highest interest rate first. It wins on paper every time.
  • Snowball: you pay the smallest balance first. It wins on morale, because you get a quick early win.

I've seen this pattern over and over in reader emails. People start the avalanche, the first card takes 14 months to clear, and they quit around month six. In real life the snowball does better than a spreadsheet says it should. If you know you'll stick with the avalanche, use it. If you're not sure, the cheaper-looking plan you abandon ends up costing more than the slightly worse plan you finish.

Automate the Investing and Stop Picking Winners

Once the buffer is full and the cards are at zero, set up an automatic transfer into a low-cost index fund the day after payday. Then leave it alone. That last step is where most people go wrong.

The numbers here are bleak for stock pickers. S&P's long-running SPIVA scorecards consistently show that the large majority of actively managed U.S. large-cap funds trail the S&P 500 over 15 years. The professionals can't do it reliably, and you don't need to try. Worse, the investors themselves often do worse than the funds they own. Morningstar's 2024 Mind the Gap study found that investors earned about 1.1 percentage points a year less than their funds over the decade, mostly because they bought and sold at the wrong times. For more on this, see The $400 Question Most Watch Collectors Can't Answer.

That gap comes from tinkering. Automation removes you from the decision, and that's the feature. If you want the fuller argument on which habits compound and which only feel productive, I made it in the finance habits that move the needle.

Watches Are Spending, Not an Asset Class

This is the misconception I fight most often on this site. A watch can hold value. Some have climbed. But treating your collection as a portfolio leads to bad decisions, because you start justifying purchases with resale fantasies. The WatchCharts market indexes have spent most of the time since their spring 2022 peak sliding back down, and plenty of people who bought at the top are still underwater.

I'm not 100% sure everyone should be as strict about this as I am... my friend Darren bought a steel sports watch at full grey-market markup in 2021, convinced it was his retirement plan. He sold it last year for $2,700 less, then spent a weekend telling everyone he'd "locked in the experience." Honestly, he wears his replacement more than he ever wore the first one, so maybe he got the better deal. I really don't know.

My rule, for what it's worth: buy watches with money that has already passed the buffer, the debt and the automatic transfer. I explain how I budget for that in a money system built for watch collectors. And if you want to know why a collection can make you feel rich while your net worth stays flat, read real wealth versus the illusion of it. For more on this, see The Money Habits I Wish I'd Built Before Buying My First Watch.

Today, open a high-yield savings account and set up an automatic transfer of a fixed amount, even $150, for the morning after your next payday. Name the account "Not a Watch." It sounds silly, but it works. You'll think twice before pulling money from an account with that name.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I have saved before buying a luxury watch?

Have at least three months of essential expenses in savings and zero credit card debt first. After that, use money left over once your automatic investing has gone out, not money set aside for bills.

Should I pay off credit cards or invest first?

Pay off any card charging more than about 15% APR first. No investment reliably beats a guaranteed 20%-plus return, and that's what clearing the balance gives you.

Are watches a good investment?

Usually not. A few models have gained value, but the secondary market has fallen a long way from its 2022 peak. Buy a watch because you'll wear it, and treat any resale value as a bonus.
