**Title:** Anyone else lose a watch they wanted because life hit at the worst possible moment?

A few years ago I had a Tudor Black Bay 58 on hold at an AD, deposit money ready, and about $1,100 in savings. Two weeks later my furnace died on a Tuesday night. The repair was $2,870, and it went on a credit card at 24 percent. The watch went to someone else.

What stuck with me wasn't the missed watch. It was that my plan only worked if nothing went wrong, and something always does. I'd budgeted for the thing I wanted and not for the thing I'd need.

Since then I've followed a boring rule: build the cash cushion first, then buy the watch. Collecting is a long game, and almost every piece you want will come around again, whether through another allocation, the pre-owned market, or a reference you like even more a year later. A surprise bill on a high-interest card can cost you more than the watch's premium ever would.

I'm curious how others handle this. Do you keep a separate watch fund, or do you treat the emergency fund as untouchable until it hits a set number? And has anyone been burned the way I was?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Should I build an emergency fund before buying a luxury watch?

Yes, in almost every case. A luxury watch is a want you can postpone, and an emergency fund is a need you can't. The order matters more than most people expect.

Here is the logic. Unexpected costs arrive on their own schedule: a failed furnace, a car repair, a medical bill, a gap between jobs. If your savings are thin when one hits, you either drain the money earmarked for the watch or you borrow, often on a credit card at 20 to 25 percent. A repair that would have been a nuisance becomes a long, expensive tail of interest.

I learned this directly. I once had a Tudor Black Bay 58 on hold at an authorized dealer with a $3,950 deposit ready and only $1,100 in savings. My furnace failed two weeks later, the repair was $2,870, and it went on a card. I lost the watch and paid interest on top.

A practical way to sequence it:

1. Build a starter buffer of roughly one month of essential expenses.
2. Work toward three to six months, depending on how stable your income is.
3. Pay off high-interest debt, since it undermines any collecting budget.
4. Set a separate, clearly labeled watch fund so the purchase never competes with your safety net.
5. Buy only when the watch can be paid for without touching the cushion.

The reassuring part is that watches are rarely gone for good. Pre-owned markets, new allocations and alternative references mean another chance usually appears. A drained emergency fund is much harder to rebuild than a missed opportunity is to replace.
